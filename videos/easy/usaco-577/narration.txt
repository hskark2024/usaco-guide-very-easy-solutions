1. High Card Low Card uses a deck numbered one through two N. Elsie owns N known cards and plays them in a fixed order. Bessie owns every card not in Elsie's hand.

2. At first, the higher card wins a point. Bessie may switch the rule once, and then the lower card wins. Switching immediately and never switching are both allowed.

3. Try every possible split. We need the best high-card score for every prefix and the best low-card score for every suffix, so we can add the two values at each boundary.

4. For a high-card round, choose Bessie's smallest available card that is strictly larger than Elsie's. It wins now and saves every larger card for harder opponents later.

5. If no card can win, this helper matching records no win and consumes nothing. Losing cards can be placed into non-scoring rounds after the winning matches are chosen.

6. The low-card greedy is the mirror image. Scanning from right to left, use the largest available Bessie card that is still strictly smaller than Elsie's card.

7. Ordered sets find both choices in logarithmic time. Prefix high scores grow left to right, while suffix low scores grow right to left.

8. In the sample, Elsie plays one, eight, four, three. Bessie owns two, five, six, seven. Winning high against one and then switching allows two more low-card wins, for three points.

9. For every split from zero through N, add prefix-high at the split to suffix-low at the same split. Take the largest sum.

10. The algorithm runs in N log N time. Exhaustive tests try every Bessie card order and every split on small games, while a fifty-thousand-round test checks the official limit.
