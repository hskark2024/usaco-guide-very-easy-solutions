3. This idea works one value at a time. If s symbols are already arranged and the next value appears c times, choose c of the new s plus c positions for those identical copies.

4. So we multiply by s plus c choose c, update s, and repeat. The product is exactly the usual multinomial coefficient, but this view is easier to compute safely.

5. The dangerous detail is the modulus. M may be composite, so factorial division and modular inverses can fail. For example, a number sharing a factor with M has no inverse modulo M.

6. Pascal's rule avoids division completely. Each binomial value is the sum of the two values above it, reduced modulo M.
