1. Polynomial Queries maintains an array. An update adds one to the first chosen position, two to the next, three to the next, and a query asks for an interval sum.

2. Updating each cell is too slow for two hundred thousand elements and commands. We need to describe the entire progression with only a few numbers.

3. Suppose an update starts at zero-based position l. At absolute position i, the added amount is i minus l plus one.

4. Rewrite that amount as one times i plus one minus l. This is an affine function: a slope times the index plus an intercept.

5. A segment-tree node knows its left and right indices. The sum of the affine function over that node is slope times the sum of its indices plus intercept times its length.

6. We can compute the index sum with the arithmetic-series formula. That lets one fully covered node absorb a whole progression update in constant time.

7. Each lazy tag stores a pending slope and intercept. Two additions combine by adding their slopes and adding their intercepts, so pushing later is safe.

8. In the sample, the array sum begins at seventeen. Updating positions one through five adds one plus two plus three plus four plus five, so the next sum is thirty-two.

9. Every position receives i minus l plus one, exactly its required place in the progression. Node sums include the update immediately, and lazy tags preserve it for descendants.

10. Building takes O of n time, and each operation takes O of log n time. Random direct simulation and a two-hundred-thousand-operation stress test verify the code and 64-bit totals.
