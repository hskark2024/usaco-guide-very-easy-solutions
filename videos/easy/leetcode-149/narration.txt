How can we find the most points on one line without trusting decimal slopes? We can turn every slope into a small pair of integers.

Pick one point as an anchor. Draw a direction vector from the anchor to every other point. If several points lie on the same line through the anchor, their vectors are scaled copies. A vector of two, three and a vector of four, six point the same way.

We remove that scale with the greatest common divisor. Divide both coordinate differences by the G C D of their absolute values. Two, three stays two, three. Four, six also becomes two, three.

There is one more detail. A line has two directions. The vectors two, three and negative two, negative three still describe the same line. We choose one official sign by making the x part positive. For a vertical line, we make the y part positive.

Now a map counts how many other points produce each normalized pair. The largest frequency plus one includes the anchor. We repeat this for every possible anchor and keep the global maximum.

Vertical lines become zero, one. Horizontal lines become one, zero. Negative slopes also have stable integer keys. No division into decimals means no rounding errors.

With at most three hundred points, checking every pair is fast enough. The method takes quadratic pair checks, with a small logarithmic map cost, and linear extra memory for one anchor.
