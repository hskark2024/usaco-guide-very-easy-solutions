1. Intersection Points gives us horizontal and vertical line segments. Our job is to count every place where one horizontal segment crosses one vertical segment.

2. Comparing every pair would take about ten billion checks when there are one hundred thousand segments. Instead, imagine moving a scanner upward across the coordinate plane.

3. A vertical segment becomes active when the scanner reaches its bottom endpoint. It stays active until the scanner reaches its top endpoint, and then we remove it.

4. When the scanner reaches a horizontal segment, a crossing happens for every active vertical whose x-coordinate lies between the horizontal segment's left and right endpoints.

5. We turn the geometry into events. A vertical creates an add event and a remove event. A horizontal creates one range-query event. Sorting by y gives the sweep order.

6. A Fenwick tree stores how many active verticals have each x-coordinate. A prefix sum counts active x-values up to one point, so subtracting two prefixes answers a whole x-range.

7. Coordinates may be negative or very large. We sort the vertical x-coordinates and replace each with its rank. Binary search converts each horizontal range into Fenwick indices.

8. In a small example, verticals at x equals two and five are both active at y equals zero. A horizontal from zero through ten crosses both, so that query contributes two.

9. Each real crossing is counted exactly once, when its horizontal query is processed. Non-crossing pairs fail either the active-y test or the x-range test.

10. Sorting and all Fenwick operations take O of n log n time and O of n memory. Random geometry tests compare against direct pair checking, and a one-hundred-thousand-segment test confirms the 64-bit answer.
