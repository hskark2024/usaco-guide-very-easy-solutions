1. Freefall asks when to stop powering up and start falling. Every power-up costs B time units, but stronger gravity makes the later fall faster. We need the earliest total arrival time.

2. After x power-ups, gravity is x plus one. The setup takes B times x, and the fall takes A divided by the square root of x plus one. Add those pieces to get our objective function.

3. The setup line always rises. The fall curve drops quickly at first, then flattens. Their sum forms one convex valley: at first a power-up may help, but eventually its fixed cost is larger than the tiny time saved.

4. We also need a finite search boundary. Doing nothing finishes at time A. If x is greater than A divided by B, the power-ups alone already cost more than A, so that choice cannot win.

5. Search integer x from zero through floor A divided by B. Place two probes at one third and two thirds. If the left value is smaller, discard the far-right part. Otherwise discard the far-left part.

6. Stop when only a handful of integers remain. Evaluate every one of them, because the true choice must be an integer and the minimum might sit between the two probes or at a boundary.

7. For A equals ten and B equals one, zero power-ups take ten. One takes about eight point zero seven, two take about seven point seven seven, and three take eight. The best integer is two.

8. For A equals five and B equals ten, even one power-up costs more than the entire original fall. The best choice is zero, and the answer is exactly five.

9. Use long double for the square root and the large products. Print many digits so the result easily meets the judge's absolute or relative error limit.

10. Each ternary step removes a constant fraction of the interval, so the algorithm takes logarithmic time and constant memory. The same pattern works whenever an integer cost decreases and then increases exactly once.
