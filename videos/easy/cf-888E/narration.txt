1. Maximum Subsequence lets us choose any subset and asks for the largest subset sum modulo m. With thirty-five values, plain subset enumeration is too slow.

2. Split the values into two halves. Enumerate every subset sum modulo m in each half, including residue zero for choosing nothing.

3. A full subset is one left choice plus one right choice. If their residues are x and y, the final result is x plus y modulo m.

4. Sort the right residues. Fix a left residue x and picture the right residues as dots from zero to m minus one.

5. To avoid wrapping, y must be at most m minus one minus x. Use upper bound to find the greatest right residue inside that limit.

6. Why can we ignore wrapped pairs? A wrapped result subtracts m and becomes smaller than x. But the empty right subset already lets us keep x, so wrapping cannot improve this fixed left choice.

7. Therefore x plus the greatest safe y is the best partner for x. Check every left residue and keep the largest result.

8. In the first sample, modulus four and values five, two, four, one can produce sum seven. Seven modulo four is three, the largest possible residue.

9. The empty subset also matters, especially when one half alone is best. It guarantees residue zero is always available for the binary search.

10. Meet in the middle reduces the work to about two to the N over two. Exhaustive random tests verify the binary-search boundary, wraparound, and modulus one.
