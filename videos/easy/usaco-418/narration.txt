1. The Lazy Cow gives us weighted grass patches. We may stand anywhere, and we want the most grass within K north, south, east, or west steps.

2. That step distance is Manhattan distance. Its reachable area looks like a diamond, which is awkward for normal rectangle data structures.

3. Rotate the coordinates by setting u equal to x plus y and v equal to x minus y. The diamond becomes an axis-aligned square.

4. A patch is reachable exactly when both transformed coordinate differences are at most K. So the square has width two K in u and in v.

5. Sort patches by u. Two pointers keep the patches whose u values fit in one width-two-K window. Each patch enters once and leaves once.

6. For an active patch at v, which center positions can reach it? Every center v zero from v minus K through v plus K works.

7. Add that patch's grass across its whole valid-center interval. A lazy segment tree handles interval additions and stores the largest total at its root.

8. In the sample, one location reaches weights three, four, and one. Their total is eight, while the weight-seven patch cannot join them.

9. Every possible optimal square appears when the sweep reaches its rightmost patch. At that moment, the tree checks every useful v center, so the best total is found.

10. Sorting and tree operations take O of N log N time. Random brute force and a one-hundred-thousand-patch stress case verify boundaries and large totals.
